The question behind my work

Why is life the way it is, and could it have been any other way?

I come at it through life's molecular machines. Their sequences hold the patterns, their structures are how they do their jobs, and enzyme kinetics at the bench tests how well they work. The microbes on my trees lived through the early Earth's changing chemistry, so their machines are the best record we have of what life needs on a young planet. That is why astrobiology sits underneath everything I do.

Evolutionary history of iron–sulfur (Fe-S) cluster enzymes

2026

I trace how enzymes built around iron–sulfur clusters, some of biology's oldest cofactors, evolved and adapted as Earth's atmosphere filled with oxygen. Oxygen damages these clusters, so their history is a record of how life coped with a changing planet.

Tools

  • Protein phylogenetics
  • Profile HMM searches
  • Ancestral state and sequence reconstruction
  • Pagel's test of correlated evolution
  • Site-directed mutagenesis
  • Cloning
  • Protein purification
  • Enzyme assays

Evolution and origins of antioxidant systems in a sulfur world

20251,000 genomes

Life has always had to live with toxic chemistry, and what counted as toxic changed with the planet. I study how molecular machines adapted to it, above all to reactive oxygen species, from the sulfur-rich early Earth to the oxygenated world that followed.

Tools

  • Species tree construction
  • Custom HMM annotation pipeline
  • Ancestral state and sequence reconstruction

Cofactors as molecular fossils: binding modes and the RNA world

2026

In 1976 Harold White proposed that many cofactors are fossils of the RNA world, nucleotide-based pieces left over from a time when RNA did the catalysis. I compare how proteins bind these cofactors and trace the binding modes through phylogenies, because the way a cofactor sits in its pocket may tell us more about that older world.

Tools

  • Binding-mode comparison
  • Protein structure analysis
  • Protein phylogenetics

Hyperspectral pigment discovery and expression in plant systems

2026400–1000 nm

A hyperspectral camera records hundreds of narrow wavebands in every pixel, where our eyes see three, so a single image of living plants can show pigment chemistry that would otherwise need a lab. I pair it with genome mining to find new pigments and express them in plants.

About

I'm an integrated PhD student in Dr. Vijay Jayaraman's lab at IISER Thiruvananthapuram, working on how molecular machines adapted as life met new chemistry. I build large phylogenies of bacteria and archaea, follow Fe-S cluster enzymes, cofactors and antioxidant systems through them, and test what the trees suggest with protein structures and enzyme work at the bench.

Most of it is computational: Python and R for analysis, SLURM for the cluster, and lately AI agents that run and check long analysis pipelines. A newer project takes me to plant pigments and hyperspectral imaging.
